Done. Overall: strong on security and uptime, weak on support SLAs, pricing sits mid-market. Three contract points need attention before renewal.

Summary of the 30-page vendor report, section by section, with page references so you can check anything that matters.

1. What the report is and how much to trust it

The report is the vendor's own annual assessment pack, prepared for existing customers who are deciding whether to renew. It combines a security and compliance overview (pages 3 to 9), an availability report (pages 10 to 14), a support and service section (pages 15 to 18), commercial terms and a price list (pages 19 to 23), a product roadmap (pages 24 to 26) and an appendix of customer references and audit letters (pages 27 to 30). Most of it is written by the vendor, so treat the claims as claims. The parts that carry outside weight are the independent auditor's letter on page 28, the penetration test summary on page 8, and the uptime figures on page 11, which are measured by a third-party monitoring service rather than by the vendor itself.

2. Security and compliance (strong)

This is the best part of the report. The vendor holds a current SOC 2 Type II report with no exceptions noted in the most recent audit period, and an ISO 27001 certificate that covers all three of its hosting regions. The auditor's letter on page 28 confirms both and lists the scope, which includes the production platform, the support tooling and the internal admin systems, not just the customer-facing product. That last point matters, because a narrow scope is a common way to make a certificate look better than the real posture.

Encryption is in line with what we would expect: data is encrypted at rest with keys managed in a hardware security module, and in transit with TLS 1.2 or higher on every external endpoint. Customer-managed keys are available, but only on the enterprise tier (page 6), which we are not on. If we want to hold our own keys, that is a pricing conversation, not a technical one.

Access control is solid. Staff access to customer data needs a ticket, a second approver and a time-limited grant, and every access is logged and kept for one year. Single sign-on and SCIM provisioning are included on our tier. Multi-factor authentication can be enforced for all of our users from the admin console.

The penetration test summary on page 8 lists two medium findings and no high or critical ones, both fixed and verified by the testing firm. The vendor runs a public bug bounty and publishes a security advisory page. The one gap in this section: the report does not say how quickly the vendor notifies customers of a breach. Page 9 says only that customers are informed "without undue delay". We should ask for a fixed notification window in the contract (see point 3 in section 10).

3. Uptime and reliability (strong)

Measured availability over the last twelve months was 99.97 percent for the core service and 99.93 percent for the reporting module (page 11), against a contractual target of 99.9 percent. There were four incidents that broke the target for a single month in any region. The longest was a 74-minute outage of the reporting module caused by a failed storage upgrade; the post-incident review is summarized on page 13 and reads as candid, with clear root causes and follow-up actions that have been completed.

The architecture section (page 12) describes active-active deployment across two availability zones in each region, with backups every hour, kept for 35 days, and copied to a second region. Restore tests are run every month, and the report gives the average restore time for a full customer environment as just under three hours. The recovery point objective is one hour and the recovery time objective is four hours. Both are reasonable for how we use the product, but they are targets, not guarantees with credits attached.

One caution: the reporting module, which our finance team leans on most heavily at month end, is the least reliable part of the platform. Three of the four incidents touched it. The vendor says a rebuilt reporting engine is in progress (page 25), but no timing is committed.

4. Support and SLAs (weak)

This is the weakest section, and the one that should shape the renewal discussion. Our current tier includes business-hours support only, in the vendor's home time zone, with these response targets (page 16):

- Severity 1 (service down): response within 4 business hours.
- Severity 2 (major function impaired): response within 1 business day.
- Severity 3 and 4: response within 3 business days.

These are response times, not resolution times. There are no resolution targets at all, and no service credits tied to support performance. In practice, a Severity 1 issue raised late in the afternoon in our time zone might not get a first reply until the next business day.

The report's own satisfaction data (page 17) shows the problem: customer satisfaction with support fell from 89 percent to 81 percent across the period, and median time to first response on Severity 2 tickets was 11 business hours, well inside the target but slow in practice. The vendor attributes the drop to growth in ticket volume and says it has hired more staff.

24 by 7 support for Severity 1 and 2 is available as an add-on, priced at 12 percent of the annual subscription (page 18), or included in the enterprise tier. A named technical account manager is only available on the enterprise tier.

The escalation path is also thin. The report names a support manager and a head of customer success as escalation contacts, but gives no rule for moving a ticket up, and no commitment to a call or a written update during a Severity 1 incident. For a tool our finance and operations teams use every day, we should ask for an update every two hours during a Severity 1 incident, and a written root-cause summary within five business days.

Our own ticket history matches the report: we raised 23 tickets over the period, two of them Severity 1, and both of those waited more than six hours for a first response because they came in outside business hours.

5. Pricing and commercial terms (mid-market)

The price list (pages 19 to 21) puts the vendor in the middle of the market for this category. Against the two alternatives we looked at during the last review, it sits roughly 15 percent above the lowest-priced option and about 20 percent below the premium option, for a comparable set of features.

Our current spend is on the professional tier, billed annually, with 250 named users and the reporting module as an add-on. List price for the same configuration at renewal rises by 7 percent, under the standard annual uplift clause. The report notes that customers renewing for two or more years can lock the price for the full term, and that multi-year renewals are eligible for a discount of 5 to 10 percent off list (page 22).

Overage pricing is the area to watch. Users above the contracted number are billed at list price per user per month, measured on the highest count in each month, not on the average. Storage above the included allowance is billed per gigabyte per month. We were close to both limits for part of the period, and we paid a small overage charge on users twice. Moving to a true-up model, where extra users are reconciled once a year at the contracted rate, would remove that surprise.

Over a two-year term, the gap between the standard uplift and a locked multi-year price is worth roughly one month of subscription, before any discount. Payment terms are net 30. There is no early payment discount. Price increases outside the renewal are allowed with 60 days' notice, capped at 3 percent (page 23), which is reasonable, but the cap only applies to the subscription and not to add-ons.

6. Product roadmap and fit (mixed)

The roadmap section (pages 24 to 26) is light on commitments. Items marked as in progress include the rebuilt reporting engine mentioned above, a public API for bulk exports, finer-grained admin roles, and a data residency option for one additional region. Items marked as under consideration include an offline mobile mode and native integrations with two more identity providers.

For our needs, the two items that matter are the bulk export API and the finer-grained admin roles. At present we export data with a scheduled report that has to be checked by hand, and admin rights are all or nothing, which forces us to give full admin to people who only need to manage users. Neither item has a committed timeline, and the report includes the usual statement that roadmap items can change. If either is a deciding factor, we should get it in writing as a commitment, or at least as a right to exit early if it does not arrive.

7. Implementation, onboarding and change management

Not a major issue for us as an existing customer, but the report (page 15) describes a standard onboarding package with a project manager, a configuration workshop and training for administrators. For existing customers adding new teams, onboarding is charged as professional services at a day rate, with a minimum of two days. The vendor publishes release notes for every change and gives 30 days' notice of any change that removes or alters an existing feature. Major interface changes can be previewed in a sandbox, which is included on our tier.

8. Data handling, privacy and exit

Data processing terms (page 7) are based on standard contractual clauses and list the vendor's sub-processors, of which there are nine, mostly hosting, email delivery and support tooling providers. The vendor commits to 30 days' notice before adding a new sub-processor, with a right to object. Data is stored in the region we selected at signup. Support staff in other regions can access it under the controls described in section 2.

Exit terms are reasonable but thin. After termination, customer data is available for export for 30 days and then deleted within a further 60 days, with a deletion certificate on request. The export format is the vendor's standard file set, which covers records and attachments but not the full audit history or the report definitions. Rebuilding reports in another tool would be a manual job. If we want a clean exit path, we should ask for the audit history and the report definitions to be included in the export, and for the export window to be extended to 90 days.

Integrations and API. The report lists the standard connectors (page 15): single sign-on, SCIM, two file storage services, a chat tool and a generic webhook. The public API is rate limited to 100 requests a minute per customer on our tier and 500 on the enterprise tier. Our nightly sync uses about a third of that limit now, so growth in users or records could push us into throttling. Worth asking whether the limit can be raised on our tier, and what the vendor does if a customer hits it repeatedly.

9. References and customer feedback

The appendix lists five reference customers (page 27), three of them in our industry and of a similar size. The quotes are positive about reliability and security and mixed about support, which is consistent with everything above. Two references mention that the account team was responsive during their renewal negotiations, and one mentions that the vendor agreed to a custom support arrangement for a higher fee. That suggests there is room to negotiate on support without moving to the enterprise tier.

Our own internal feedback, from the short survey of the 40 most active users, is broadly in line: users rate the product well for day-to-day work, the finance team is frustrated with the reporting module, and admins want the finer-grained roles.

Scorecard, as the report supports it:
- Security and compliance: strong. Independent audit, broad scope, clean penetration test, one gap on breach notice.
- Uptime and reliability: strong for the core service, weaker for the reporting module.
- Support: weak. Business hours only on our tier, response targets without resolution targets, falling satisfaction.
- Pricing: mid-market, fair for the features, with an overage model that costs us more than it should.
- Roadmap: thin. Nothing we need is committed.
- Data handling and exit: adequate, with gaps in what the export includes.
- References: consistent with the above, and a sign that support terms are negotiable.

10. The three contract points that need attention before renewal

Point 1: support terms. Ask for 24 by 7 coverage for Severity 1 issues, a resolution or workaround target of 8 hours for Severity 1, and service credits if those targets are missed. The add-on price of 12 percent is a starting point. A reasonable ask is to include Severity 1 coverage at no extra cost in exchange for a two-year term, or at 5 to 6 percent of the subscription.

Point 2: price protection and overages. Lock the price for the full term with a multi-year renewal, push for the 10 percent multi-year discount, extend the 3 percent cap on mid-term increases to add-ons, and move to an annual true-up for users at the contracted rate instead of monthly overage at list price.

Point 3: data and exit. Set a fixed breach notification window of 72 hours, include the audit history and report definitions in the export, extend the export window to 90 days, and add a right to terminate early, without penalty, if the bulk export API is not delivered within a reasonable period.

11. Risks and open questions

- Support capacity: satisfaction fell over the period. Ask what staffing changes have been made and request the latest monthly figures for time to first response.
- Reporting module reliability: three of the four incidents touched it. Ask for the target availability of the rebuilt engine and whether credits will apply to the module separately.
- Roadmap: no committed timing for the two items we care about. Ask for a written commitment or an exit right tied to them.
- Customer-managed keys: only on the enterprise tier. Confirm whether our security team needs them in the next term. If yes, compare the cost of moving up a tier with the cost of the add-ons we would otherwise buy.
- Sub-processors: nine at present, with a 30-day notice period. Confirm that our privacy team has reviewed the current list.

12. Recommendation and next steps

Renew, but do not accept the standard terms. The security posture and measured uptime are strong enough that switching would cost us more than it saves, and the pricing is fair for what we get. The weak support terms and the overage model are real costs that we can reduce through negotiation, and the references suggest the vendor is willing to move on support.

Suggested next steps:
1. Share this summary with procurement, security, finance and the main admin team, and confirm the three contract points.
2. Ask the vendor for the latest support metrics, the target availability of the rebuilt reporting engine, and a written position on the bulk export API.
3. Request a multi-year renewal quote with the support, overage and exit changes above, and a single-year quote for comparison.
4. Ask legal to review the data processing terms and the exit clause against our standard positions.
5. Hold the renewal decision until the revised quote arrives, and keep the comparison with the two alternatives current in case the vendor will not move.
